## What an A-Corp Is (Yancey's definition, verbatim)

> "An Artist Corporation is a new company form designed to protect creative people and creative work by default."

Legally: a **new variant of the LLC**, created by the Colorado Artist Companies Act (SB 26-133, signed June 2026), that starts from creative purpose, artist control, shared ownership, and IP - instead of starting from a generic business structure and paying lawyers to bend it.

## Where It Came From

Yancey's own account, condensed:

- Son of a musician in southwest Virginia; music was identity, not income
- Music journalist (Pitchfork, Spin, Village Voice), ran a small record label
- Co-founded **Kickstarter** with Perry Chen and Charles Adler; Kickstarter became a **Public Benefit Corporation in 2015**, one of the first to adopt the Delaware structure. **The A-Corp is explicitly modeled on the PBC precedent** - "Could we do for creative people what the Public Benefit Corporation had done for mission-driven companies?"
- Stepped down as CEO in 2017
- Hit the problem personally: a multi-writer book collection with no entity representing the group; forming an LLC that reflected how they worked required a custom lawyer-built structure
- A friend working with prominent visual and performing artists confirmed the pattern - artists paying for bespoke legal structures that were, in effect, all the same thing
- A group of lawyers led by **Susan Mac Cormac** (with associate **Stephanie Drumm**) drafted the statute

## What Creative People Told Them

Research base: interviews across fields plus an online survey with **more than 1,600 responses**.

Consistent themes:
- Hard to find funding
- Legal and administrative work expensive and confusing
- Collaborators lack clarity about ownership
- Powerlessness inside systems they do not control

Biggest pain points ranked: **access to funding, clarity of ownership, administrative burden, access to healthcare and benefits.**

The surprise finding, worth internalizing at ZAO:

> "We were surprised to find that special tax treatment was less important to respondents than becoming more capable of accepting investment. People did not want to be permanently reliant on charity. They wanted tools that could help them become more independent and self-sufficient."

Supporting stat from artistcorporations.com: **85% of artists earn less than $25,000/yr, and only ~13% earn a full-time living from their practice.** "That isn't a talent problem - it's a structural one."

## The Four Built-In Provisions (plus a fifth possibility)

| # | Provision | Detail |
|---|-----------|--------|
| 1 | **51% artist control** | The company must be at least 51% owned and controlled by artists or creators. Locked into the statute - **cannot be changed by an operating agreement.** Outside investors, non-artist partners, and operational/financial/technical expertise are all allowed; majority ownership and control are not transferable. "Spotify could not declare itself an Artist Corporation simply because it operates in the music industry." |
| 2 | **Stated artistic mission** | Declared in founding documents and carrying legal weight. The company can specify that the mission has **primacy over financial objectives**, that they are equal, or define its own balance. Purpose covers create, develop, produce, distribute, exhibit, perform, or otherwise support creative work |
| 3 | **IP and reversionary rights** | Artistic work contributed to an A-Corp is protected. **If the company dissolves, the work returns to the artists who made it** - and these reversionary rights are not available to creditors. Artistic work can never be transferred to non-artist investors or third parties |
| 4 | **A-Corp Shares (fractional ownership)** | A new ownership unit. Shares can be issued to artists in exchange for creative contributions - **creative work is recognized as a capital contribution with real value, not sweat equity.** Collaborators, supporters, and investors participate in upside while artists keep voting control. Investment still follows federal securities law |
| 5 | **Shared benefits (not created by the law)** | If A-Corps reach meaningful adoption, they could enable group healthcare or other employment support. "Individually we're rounding errors inside systems designed for someone else." The law creates a possible foundation, not the outcome |

## What an A-Corp Does NOT Do

This is the section most summaries drop. Recorded in full because it is where ZAO's expectations should be set.

- **It does not make anything newly possible.** "With enough money and the right lawyers, creative people have already been able to build structures containing many of these provisions." What it does is make the *beginning* cheaper and more accessible
- **It does not eliminate lawyers.** Simple A-Corps should form from standard documents; significant investment, multi-country work, municipal funding, unusual ownership, or complex IP negotiation still needs counsel
- **It does not change federal tax law** and creates **no new deduction**
- **It does not change securities law.** You still cannot publicly solicit anyone to invest without the appropriate regulatory process
- **It cannot receive tax-deductible philanthropic donations directly.** That still requires a fiscal sponsor or nonprofit partner. A federal-level pathway has been explored but does not exist
- **It does not automatically create funding, healthcare, or financial success**

Yancey's own risk framing:

> "There's a version of the future where the law exists, few people use it, and none of its larger potential is realized... The law cannot create that future by itself. Creative people have to choose it, use it, and have success with it."

## Q&A Highlights

**What counts as an artist?** The statute draws its definition from existing copyright-law language: written, visual, graphic, literary, musical, audio, digital, performing, interactive, and other creative work. "We tried to write a 21st-century definition without making it so broad that every business could call itself an A-Corp." **In the beginning, people will self-attest.** Stronger anti-abuse protections may follow if material financial or tax benefits attach later.

**Can an A-Corp take investment?** Yes, under the same federal securities law as any company. First phase focuses on smaller-scale structuring: close networks, professional cultural investors. **No prescribed investment model** - royalty, profit share, project-revenue share, or studio equity are all expressible.

**Does it change how creative work is valued?** No. "Large financial institutions know how to acquire music catalogs and treat them as valuable assets. Individual artists often lack the legal and financial infrastructure to use their work in the same way." The open question is whether a standardized structure makes creative assets legible **without forcing artists to sell them outright** - which is precisely the ZAO thesis.

**Grants and investment together?** Investment yes; direct tax-deductible philanthropy no.

## The Tooling

**artistcorporations.com** hosts a sample registration process that turns the statute into plain questions:

1. What is the creative mission of the company?
2. How should financial returns and creative purpose relate to one another?
3. Who are the founding members?
4. How should ownership be divided?
5. What creative work belongs to the A-Corp?

The site also runs a financial calculator, a structure comparison, a pre-registration list, and an AI Q&A answering "from the law itself" (their disclaimer: not legal advice). Press coverage cited on the site: The Art Newspaper, Hyperallergic, NPR, Frieze.

**Those five questions are the deliverable for ZAO.** They are the exact questions ZAO has never systematically asked incoming artists, and they can be lifted into artist onboarding today with no statute involved.
