## The 5-Component Positioning Framework

April Dunford's methodology rests on a structured approach with 5 key components that together define how a market understands your product. Each component answers a specific question about your offering and its place in the customer's world:

### 1. Competitive Alternatives

**What the prospect would do if your product didn't exist.**

This is the critical starting point. Positioning is not about competing with your feature list - it's about understanding what alternatives your customer is already considering or using. The alternative might be a manual process ("doing it by hand"), buying a different product category, building it in-house, or doing nothing at all.

**Why it matters:** Your strengths only matter if they matter MORE than what the customer would otherwise do. Dunford's example from her consulting work: a CRM repositioning focused on "easier data entry" until a client recognized the real alternative was "using a spreadsheet" - the positioning shifted to "centralized customer data," which was what customers actually valued.

**ZAO Mapping - Example: ZAOstock Sponsor Pitch (local venue/festival sponsors)**
- **Competitive alternative for a local Maine sponsor (vs. ZAOstock):** A Facebook ad campaign, a Nextdoor banner ad, a local chamber-of-commerce newsletter listing, or a bake-sale/fundraiser. NOT other festivals - the sponsor's buying decision is "how do we reach local supporters" in the summer season.

### 2. Unique Attributes

**What you do that the competitive alternatives don't (or can't).**

Attributes are facts about your product, service, or approach that differentiate you. They are concrete and defensible. The art is separating the truly unique (hard to copy) from the "me too" (which competitors match within months).

Dunford emphasizes: attribute alone is not positioning. A feature becomes valuable only in context - when it solves a problem the customer cares about AND the alternative lacks it.

### 3. Value

**What the prospect will be able to do, achieve, or avoid as a result of using you instead of the alternative.**

Value is outcome-focused, not feature-focused. It answers "so what?" - so what if your product is faster, cheaper, or easier? What does the customer actually achieve?

Dunford distinguishes:
- **Functional value:** Task gets done faster, cheaper, with fewer errors.
- **Emotional value:** Feel more confident, less anxious, more in control.
- **Strategic value:** Unlocks a new capability, opens a new market, or enables a new business model.

The strongest positioning connects to strategic value when it exists.

### 4. Target Segment

**Which customers benefit most from this value in this way.**

Positioning without a target segment is positioning for nobody. A strong segment is specific enough that the positioning makes sense to them immediately. It's not "everyone" - it's "solo musicians on Farcaster who want community investment" or "festival sponsors who want brand lift beyond ads."

Dunford emphasizes: you can serve many segments, but each needs its OWN positioning statement. Trying to be everything to everyone is the death of positioning.

### 5. Market Category

**What category does the prospect think about when they think about solving this problem?**

This is about framing. Is your product a "CRM" or a "customer intelligence platform"? A "newsletter" or "audience builder"? The category anchors how prospects evaluate you against alternatives.

Strong positioning often involves category reframing - moving a customer's mental model from one category (where you are weak) to another (where you are strong). Dunford's canonical example: a SQL database repositioned not as "database" but as "operational analytics platform," shifting the competitive set from Oracle/Postgres to Tableau/Looker.

## The Process: How to Build Positioning

Dunford's method is a structured 10-step process (outline from "Obviously Awesome"):

1. **List the alternatives** - What would customers do if your product didn't exist? Brainstorm the full list.
2. **List your unique attributes** - What are you genuinely good at that alternatives are not?
3. **Map attributes to value** - For each attribute, ask: "So what? What does this let customers do?"
4. **Determine your (likely) economic buyer** - Who makes the buying decision?
5. **Determine the context** - Why would they care NOW?  (Problem, trigger, budget available, urgent need.)
6. **Identify segment-specific value** - Different buyer personas value different things. Map the value that matters most to YOUR best-fit customer.
7. **Define the market category** - What mental bucket is your customer in? (Is this a "CRM" or "customer intelligence platform"?)
8. **Test and iterate** - Share your positioning with target customers. Do they intuitively "get" it?
9. **Define the customer problem statement** - In one sentence, the problem you solve for this segment in this context.
10. **Build the positioning statement** - Synthesize: "For [segment], [positioning is unique in that it] [value], unlike [alternative]."

### ZAO Application Example: The ZAOstock Local Sponsor Positioning

Applying the 10 steps to ZAOstock local sponsor pitch deck (Segment 2):

1. **Alternatives:** Facebook ad campaign ($500-2k/summer), Nextdoor banner, chamber newsletter, local partnership with tourism bureau, bake-sale fundraiser.
2. **Unique attributes (ZAOstock):** Creator-owned (no labels), token-free (no speculation), two-stage live event (music non-stop), local after-party at Black Moon, artist-led (not promoter-led), Decentraland mirror (hybrid IRL+digital).
3. **Value to sponsor:** Brand lift (association with authentic music community), guaranteed foot traffic (underwriter of a day-long event), secondary value from Black Moon gift cards + attendee list, built-in UGC/content capture (photos/gallery), measurable local-business lift (iPad stations at Black Moon tracking transactions).
4. **Economic buyer:** Small business owner or tourism director with summer-season discretionary marketing budget ($1-5k spend).
5. **Context:** Summer season (June-Sept) is peak visitor + local spending. Ellsworth has two beaches, farm stands, galleries. Previous summer activations (Chesnee/Heart of Ellsworth festival, doc 2279) showed demand.
6. **Segment value:** Local sponsor = "I want to reach summer visitors and locals in one shot, with association to something genuine (not corporate)." Value is reach + authenticity + ROI proof.
7. **Category:** Not "festival sponsorship" (already saturated) but "summer pop-up brand activation" or "community event co-marketing."
8. **Test:** Candy (pitch-deck lead per doc 2310) will test the local variant with 3-5 Ellsworth businesses. Feedback should answer: "Does this make sense for MY business's summer goals?"
9. **Problem statement:** "Local Maine businesses want to reach summer visitors and residents with authentic brand association but have no proven summer channel except ads."
10. **Positioning:** "For Ellsworth-area small businesses, ZAOstock is the one summer activation that puts your brand in front of [visitor + local audience] through music + community, unlike Facebook ads or chamber listings."

## Dunford's Core Principles

Beyond the framework, Dunford emphasizes:

- **Context-setting over feature lists.** A feature is only valuable in context. "Faster database queries" means nothing without knowing: faster than what alternative, for which customer, solving what problem?
- **Boring positioning wins.** The best positioning is often obvious once found, because it correctly reflects reality. "We are better at this, for this customer, than the alternative" - not a clever turn of phrase, but a truthful statement.
- **Positioning cascades.** Company positioning flows into product messaging, which flows into sales materials, which flows into ad copy. If positioning is strong, everything downstream is clearer.
- **Revisit often.** Markets shift. Competitors emerge. Customers' mental models change. Positioning should be revisited quarterly or whenever a major competitive threat or segment shift occurs.

## Applying Dunford to New Positioning Work

For any ZAO brand or product entering a positioning phase:

**Use the 5 components as the diagnostic:**
1. If you can't name the competitive alternative, your positioning is weak. (You're fighting invisible competition.)
2. If your unique attribute doesn't connect to a customer value, it's a feature, not positioning.
3. If your segment is "everyone," your positioning is "no one."
4. If your category reframe doesn't shift the competitive set, it's not a reframe - it's a description.
5. If you can't state your positioning in one sentence, it's not tight enough to ship in pitch decks or ads.

**Dunford's test:** Can a stranger who knows your space read your positioning statement and immediately think "oh, THAT makes sense for THAT customer over THAT alternative"? If not, tighten.
